# Worked example: a quick answer or independent understanding

Albert Toma · Supplement to the basic Book concept

This is a hypothetical example, not a product trial report.

## Intent

Help a learner solve unfamiliar problems of a defined kind independently. Here, independence means being able to explain the reasoning and solve a new comparable problem without being given the answer.

## Context of the Author and Organization

In this hypothetical example, the author explains: "Good learning means that a person can apply knowledge independently." The speed of receiving an answer is therefore not sufficient evidence of quality. This interpretation rests on the author's direct explanation; if reconstructed only from earlier materials, it remains an assumption pending clarification.

If a company runs the workshop, its Book separately records the adopted learning goal and who may change it. An employee's personal wish to help participants faster may be considered as a proposal, but it does not replace the company's goal. The User's Book and Company Book may be combined in one document provided each proposition is clearly attributed.

## A decision and possible drift

The team adds a button that reveals the answer and measures the time taken to obtain it. That time decreases. The feature succeeds by this measure, but its effect on independence remains unknown. Substitution occurs if speed alone is treated as sufficient evidence that the original goal has been achieved.

## Questions to clarify

- Who is the product for, and which problems should it address?
- When does a complete answer help, and when does it replace an independent attempt?
- What evidence would show transfer of understanding to a new problem?
- Who may change the goal if the team decides to build an answer service?

## A possible correction

Ask for an initial attempt and explanation, then offer a gradual hint and discussion. The complete answer may remain available: what matters is a testable connection between the decision and the goal. This proposal requires evaluation; it does not itself establish an improvement in learning.

## How to evaluate it

Before the trial, define the problem class, permitted assistance, and criterion for independent success. Compare performance on new comparable problems, accounting for participants' initial differences, time, and need for hints. Choose the trial design and size according to the expected effect and available resources. One successful episode does not justify a general conclusion.

## What to retain in the Book

The intent and definition of independence; grounds for choosing hints; alternatives; the adopted decision; evaluation conditions; observations; and limits of the conclusion. If the result differs from expectations, reconsider dependent decisions. If the goal changes, record its new meaning and the grounds for the change.

## Aging and obsolescence

Even with an unchanged goal, errors, ambiguities, and unresolved contradictions may accumulate in the materials. This is aging of the working state. Changes in external conditions may instead make information obsolete. Both require review, but their causes differ. Corrections to a record should be checked against dependent decisions and actual results.
